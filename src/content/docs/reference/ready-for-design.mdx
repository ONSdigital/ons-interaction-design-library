Before we start designing, we need enough understanding of the problem, the people we are designing for, and the context in which the design needs to work.

**Ready for Design is a lightweight checkpoint that helps us establish this shared understanding before significant design work begins.**

It does not mean that everything must be known or that discovery is complete. New information will continue to emerge as we design, test and iterate.

It simply means:

> **We know enough to start, and we know what we do not yet know.**

## Why we need this

When we start designing too early, important information can emerge after considerable work has already taken place.

This can mean:

- designs are based on assumptions rather than evidence
- important user needs are discovered late
- business, service or technical requirements emerge during design
- substantial redesign becomes necessary
- time is spent investigating fundamental questions instead of solving design problems
- implementation begins before important decisions have been made

Late changes can also affect research that has already taken place.

If a new requirement significantly changes the design, previous usability testing may no longer represent the revised experience. Findings may become less relevant or, in some cases, the design may need to be tested again.

This costs additional time, money and resource that could have been avoided by identifying important requirements earlier.

Ready for Design helps us reduce this risk by making sure we understand enough before investing significant time in a design direction.

## What we need to understand

Before significant design work begins, the team should be able to answer six questions.

### 1. What problem are we trying to solve?

We can explain the problem or opportunity, rather than only describing a requested feature or proposed solution.

Where appropriate, we should also have an idea of what a successful outcome would look like.

### 2. Who are we designing for?

We understand the relevant users or audiences and have some evidence about their needs, behaviours or problems.

### 3. What does the organisation need?

We understand the important product, business, service, content or publishing needs that the work must support.

### 4. What do we already know?

We have considered the evidence that already exists.

Depending on the work, this might include:

- user research
- analytics or performance data
- previous designs or research
- existing journeys and content
- feedback or support enquiries
- workshops
- technical investigation
- input from subject-matter experts

Not every piece of work will need every type of evidence.

### 5. What could affect the design?

We understand the important constraints and dependencies that are already known.

These might include technical feasibility, accessibility, content, data, business rules, policy, other services or dependencies on other teams.

### 6. What do we not know yet?

Known gaps, assumptions and unanswered questions are visible.

We do not need every question to be answered before Design begins.

However, if an unanswered question could significantly change the direction of the design, someone should be responsible for resolving it.

## The Ready for Design checkpoint

A piece of work is likely to be Ready for Design when:

- we understand the problem
- we understand who we are designing for
- we have enough evidence to start
- important requirements are understood
- significant constraints and dependencies are known
- important unknowns and assumptions are visible

Then ask:

> **Could something we do not currently understand fundamentally change the direction or validity of the design?**

If **yes**, more discovery or refinement may be needed.

If **no**, the work is probably **Ready for Design**.

## Keep it proportionate

Ready for Design should be proportionate to the size, complexity and risk of the work.

A small change to an established component or pattern might only require reviewing existing evidence and confirming the requirement with the relevant people.

A large or complex piece of work may need user research, analytics, workshops, technical investigation and input from subject-matter experts.

Ready for Design does not require every piece of work to go through the same activities.

The aim is simply to make a considered decision about whether we understand enough to start.

## How it works in practice

Ready for Design should form part of our normal backlog refinement and design workflow. It should not require a separate approval process or large amounts of additional documentation.

### 1. Work enters the backlog

Ideas, problems and requests can still enter the backlog before everything is understood.

The backlog does not need to contain work that is ready to be designed.

### 2. Discovery and refinement happens

Before significant design work begins, the relevant people work together to fill important gaps in our understanding.

What this involves will depend on the work. It might be as simple as a conversation and reviewing existing evidence, or it might require further research, analysis or technical investigation.

This should happen early enough that significant questions are not being discovered after Design has already committed to a direction.

### 3. The Jira ticket is refined

As we learn more, the Jira ticket should contain enough information for the team to understand the work.

Supporting research and documentation can be linked rather than copied into Jira.

### 4. Work moves to Ready for Design

Once the team believes there is enough understanding to begin, the ticket can move to a **Ready for Design** column on the Jira board.

This creates a useful distinction:

> **The Backlog represents work we may need to do. Ready for Design represents work we understand well enough to start designing.**

### 5. Interaction Design begins

Interaction Designers can normally pick up new work from **Ready for Design**, rather than beginning significant design work directly from an unrefined backlog.

Discovery does not stop at this point.

Designing, prototyping and testing will continue to teach us things. If something fundamental emerges, it is OK to pause, investigate it and reconsider the direction.

## What should be in the Jira ticket?

We should keep this lightweight.

The Jira ticket should give the designer enough context to understand the work and provide links to supporting evidence where needed.

As a minimum, consider capturing:

### Problem

What problem are we trying to solve?

### Users and evidence

Who are we designing for, and what evidence do we have about their needs?

### Requirements

What important product, business, service, content, data or technical requirements do we already know about?

### Constraints and dependencies

What could affect the design?

### Unknowns and assumptions

What do we not know yet?

Could any of these unanswered questions significantly change the direction of the design?

### Supporting information

Link to relevant research, analytics, workshops, documentation, previous designs or other useful evidence.

## Ready for Design is a shared responsibility

Ready for Design should not be a form that one person fills out and hands over to Interaction Design.

The relevant people should work together to understand the problem and identify important gaps.

Depending on the work, this could involve Product, Interaction Design, User Research, Performance Analysis, Content Design, Business Analysis, technical colleagues and subject-matter experts.

The people involved will vary. The important thing is that we have the right knowledge and evidence available to make a sensible decision.

Ready for Design is not about removing all uncertainty or creating another approval process.

It is about asking one simple question before we invest significant time in designing a solution:

> **Do we understand this well enough to start making meaningful design decisions?**
