---
title: Ready for Design
description: A lightweight checkpoint to make sure we understand enough about a piece of work before Interaction Design begins.
---

Interaction Design should not start with a blank canvas and a Jira ticket containing a proposed solution.

Before we begin designing, we need enough understanding of the problem, the people we are designing for, and the context in which the design needs to work.

**Ready for Design is a lightweight checkpoint that helps us establish this shared understanding before significant design work begins.**

It does not mean that everything must be known or that discovery is complete. New information will continue to emerge as we design, test and iterate.

It means:

> **We know enough to start, and we know what we do not yet know.**

## Why we need this

When design starts before a problem has been sufficiently understood, important information can emerge after considerable work has already taken place.

This can mean:

- designs are based on assumptions rather than evidence
- important user needs are discovered late
- business, service or technical requirements emerge during design
- substantial redesign becomes necessary
- designers spend time investigating fundamental product or service questions
- different people make different assumptions about how a service works
- implementation begins before important decisions have been made

Late changes can also undermine research that has already taken place.

If a significant new requirement changes the design, previous usability testing may no longer represent the revised experience. The findings may become less relevant or, in some cases, no longer be valid. The design may then need to be tested again, using additional time, money and research capacity.

Ready for Design helps us identify these risks earlier. It allows Interaction Designers to spend more of their time solving design problems and less time uncovering fundamental requirements by accident.

## What we need to understand

Before significant design work begins, the team should be able to answer the following questions.

### What problem are we trying to solve?

We can explain the problem or opportunity rather than only describing a requested feature or proposed solution.

### Who are we designing for?

We know the relevant users or audiences and have some evidence about their needs, behaviours or problems.

### What does the organisation need?

We understand the important product, business, service or publishing needs that the work must support.

### What do we already know?

Relevant evidence has been gathered and shared. Depending on the work, this might include:

- user research
- analytics
- previous design or research work
- feedback and support enquiries
- workshops
- technical investigation
- content analysis
- input from subject-matter experts

### What constraints and dependencies do we know about?

Important factors that could affect the design have been identified. These might include:

- technical constraints
- content and data requirements
- accessibility considerations
- policy or legal requirements
- established business or service rules
- dependencies on other teams, services or systems
- relevant Design System components or existing Website Design Library guidance

### What do we not know yet?

Known gaps, assumptions and unanswered questions are visible.

Where an unanswered question could significantly affect the scope or direction of the design, someone should be responsible for resolving it.

## The Ready for Design checkpoint

A piece of work is likely to be Ready for Design when:

- the problem is understood
- the relevant users and their needs are understood
- there is enough evidence to begin
- important business and service requirements are understood
- significant constraints and dependencies have been identified
- known assumptions and unanswered questions are visible
- important unanswered questions have an owner

The team should then ask:

> **Could something we do not currently understand fundamentally change the direction or validity of the design?**

If the answer is **no**, the work can move into Design.

If the answer is **yes**, more discovery may be needed before significant design work begins.

## Keep it proportionate

Ready for Design should be proportionate to the complexity, uncertainty and risk of the work.

A small change to an established component or pattern may only require a conversation, a review of existing evidence and confirmation of the requirement.

A new or complex service experience may require more substantial discovery, such as:

- user research
- stakeholder or subject-matter expert workshops
- analysis of existing content and data
- technical investigation
- mapping business or service processes
- identifying variations and exceptions
- testing assumptions with users

Ready for Design does not require every piece of work to go through the same discovery activities. It requires the team to make a considered decision about whether it understands enough to begin.

## Recording this in Jira

The Jira ticket does not need to contain every detail. It should give the designer enough context to understand the work and link to supporting evidence where needed.

As a minimum, it should capture:

### Problem

What problem are we trying to solve?

### Users and evidence

Who are we designing for, what do we know about their needs, and what evidence supports this?

### Requirements

What important product, business, service, content, data or technical requirements must the work support?

### Constraints and dependencies

What could constrain or affect the design?

### Unknowns and assumptions

What do we not know yet? What assumptions are we making, and who is responsible for resolving important unanswered questions?

### Supporting information

Link to relevant research, analytics, workshop outputs, technical documentation, previous designs or other supporting evidence.

## Discovery continues during Design

Ready for Design does not prevent designers from discovering new information.

Interaction Design is iterative. Research, prototyping and usability testing will continue to reveal opportunities, questions and unexpected user needs.

There is, however, a difference between:

- discovering something through the normal process of designing and testing a solution
- discovering a fundamental unresolved product, business or service question that could invalidate the direction of the work

If a fundamental question emerges, it is appropriate to pause and assess it rather than continue designing around an assumption.

The question should be documented and assigned to the appropriate product, business, service, content or technical owner. Interaction Design can support this work, but should not automatically become responsible for resolving decisions that sit outside Design.

Design can resume once there is enough clarity to continue.

## Ready for Design is a shared responsibility

Ready for Design is not a form that one person completes before handing work over to an Interaction Designer.

It should be a shared conversation involving the relevant people. Depending on the work, this could include:

- Product
- Interaction Design
- User Research
- Content Design
- Business Analysis
- technical colleagues
- subject-matter experts
- service or business owners

The value comes from identifying gaps and assumptions together, not from completing a checklist.

Ready for Design should help the team make a shared decision:

> **Do we understand this well enough for Interaction Design to start making meaningful design decisions?**

The intention is not to slow delivery down or remove all uncertainty. It is to reduce avoidable risk, prevent unnecessary rework and make better use of design, research and delivery resources.
