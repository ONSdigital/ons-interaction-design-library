Ready for Design is a checkpoint that ensures work has been sufficiently understood and defined before Interaction Design begins.

It provides a shared understanding of:
* The problem we are trying to solve
* The users and their needs
* The scope of the work
* Relevant business and service rules
* Known variations and exceptions
* Content and data requirements
* Dependencies
* Outstanding decisions and their owners
* What success looks like

The purpose is not to eliminate uncertainty or prevent discovery.

Instead, it is to ensure that fundamental product, service and business questions are identified and owned before Interaction Design is expected to produce a solution.

## 1. Why do we need this?

Design works best when designers have a clear understanding of the problem they are being asked to solve.

When fundamental information is missing, Interaction Design can unintentionally become the place where product, business and service questions are discovered and resolved.

This creates several risks:
* Design work takes longer than expected.
* Interaction Designers spend time investigating questions that should have been resolved earlier.
* Different people may make different assumptions about how a service works.
* Designs may need to be substantially changed when new information emerges.
* Inconsistent business or service practices can become embedded in new design.
* Designers can become responsible for resolving questions they do not own.
* Delivery teams may begin implementation before important decisions have been made.

Ready for Design aims to move these questions upstream where possible.

## 2. Example: Dataset work

The Dataset work provides an example of why this is important.

During the development of the Dataset experience, Interaction Design uncovered significant variation in how datasets were structured and published.

For example:
* Some datasets use editions while other use versions in different ways
* Some datasets have a single edition with versions representing corrections
* Datasets can be ordered chronologically in some circumstances
* Other datasets are grouped or ordered by geography
* Some datasets can have multiple current or latest editions because data for England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland is released separately
* Approached to historical releases are not always consistent

These differences have a direct impact on the design of navigation, presentation and reusable templates.

The difficulty was not simply that information was missing from a design brief.

Some of these questions had not been identified as questions that needed answering before Interaction Design began.

As a result, Interaction Design became responsible for discovering how the service worked while simultaneously trying to design a consistent and reusable experience.

This type of discovery is valuable, but it can significantly increase the complexity and duration of Interaction Design work.

Ready for Design provides a mechanism for identifying this type of uncertainty earlier, where reasonably possible, and ensuring that unresolved questions have clear ownership.

## 3. What Ready for Design means

Ready for Design does not mean that everything is known or that discovery is finished.

Interaction Design is an iterative process and new information will always emerge.

Ready for Design does not mean:
* Every requirement is completely defined
* Every edge case has been resolved
* The solution has already been designed
* Design cannot challenge existing requirements
* No further discovery will be necessary
* All uncertainty has been removed

Ready for Design does mean:
* The problem is understood sufficiently to begin
* The user need is understood
* The scope is understood
* Relevant business and service rules are documented
* Important domain concepts and terminology are understood
* Known variations and exceptions have been identified
* Dependencies and constraints are understood
* Outstanding decisions have identified owners
* There is sufficient information for Interaction Design to make meaningful process

There standard is therefore:
> We know enough to start Design, and we know what we do not yet know.

## 4. What needs to be established?

The following areas should be considered before work is accepted into Design.

### 4.1 User need

We should understand:
* Who are the users?
* What are they trying to achieve?
* What problem or need are we addressing?
* What evidence do we have for this need?
* What outcome are we trying to achieve for users?

The relevant user needs and evidence should be documented or linked from the Jira item.

### 4.2 Problem and outcome

The problem should be clearly described without prescribing a design solution.

We should understand:
* What problem are we solving?
* Why does it need to be solved?
* What happens if we do nothing?
* What does success look like?

This helps ensure that Interaction Design is solving the underlying problem rather than simply implementing a predetermined solution.

### 4.3 Scope

The scope of the work should be sufficiently clear.

This should include:
* What is included
* What is out of scope
* Which services, journeys or content are affected
* Known dependencies
* Related work that may affect the outcome

### 4.4 Business and service rules

The service or business rules that could affect the user experience should be understood and documented.

This may include:
* Eligibility rules
* Required information
* Validation rules
* Business processes
* Rules governing different user types
* Content requirements
* Legal or policy requirements
* Relationships between important concepts
* Rules governing different states or outcomes

Where relevant, important domain concepts should have agreed definitions.

For example:
What is a dataset?
What is an edition
What is a version?
What is a release?
How do these concepts relate to one another?

Interaction Design should not be expected to infer fundamental service rules from existing interfaces.

### 4.5 Known variations and exceptions

Existing services may not always operate consistently.

Before Design begins, we should identify known variations such as:
* different publishing processes
* different content structures
* different data structures
* different user journeys
* legacy behaviour
* geographic or organisational differences
* legitimate exceptions to standard processes

Where variations exist, we should understand whether the new service should:
* accommodate the variation
* standardise the variation
* remove the variation
* treat it as out of scope

If this decision has not been made, the decision owner should be identified.

### 4.6 Content and data

Where relevant, we should understand:
* what content is required
* what data is required
* where the data or content comes from
* who owns it
* whether realistic examples are available
* whether there are known variations in structure or quality

Design should not be expected to create a reusable solution without understanding significant variations in the content or data it needs to represent.

### 4.7 Technical and service constraints

Design does not need a technical solution before it starts.

However, known constraints that could materially affect the design should be identified.

These might include:
* existing systems or services
* technical dependencies
* data availability
* integration requirements
* accessibility requirements
* security or privacy constraints
* platform limitations
* implementation constraints

### 4.8 User stories and acceptance criteria

Where Agile delivery is being used, the relevant User Stories should be available and sufficiently understood.

Acceptance criteria should describe what needs to be true for the work to be considered successful.

User Stories and acceptance criteria should describe the required user or service outcome rather than prescribing a specific interface solution.

### 4.9 Decisions and ownership

Any outstanding decisions should be documented.

For each significant unresolved question, identify:

**Question:**
What needs to be decided?

**Impact:**
Could the answer materially affect Design?

**Owner:**
Who is responsible for making the decision?

**Required by:**
When does the decision need to be made?

This makes uncertainty visible rather than allowing it to become an implicit responsibility of the designer.

## 5. The Ready for Design checkpoint

Once the required information has been assembled, the work should be reviewed in a Design Readiness Check.

The purpose of the meeting is not to design the solution.

It is to answer:

**Is this work sufficiently understood and defined for Design to begin?**

The discussion should focus on:
1. What problem are we solving?
2. Who are the users?
3. What do we know about their needs?
4. What does the service currently do?
5. What business and service rules apply?
6. What variations or exceptions exist?
7. What is in and out of scope?
8. What dependencies and constraints exist?
9. What decisions remain outstanding?
10. Could any of those decisions materially affect Design?

## 6. Outcomes of the checkpoint

The checkpoint should have three possible outcomes.

🟢 **Ready for Design**
The work is sufficiently understood and can enter the Design process.

🟠 **Ready with actions**
The work can begin, but there are minor outstanding actions that have clear owners and are not expected to prevent meaningful Design progress.

🔴 **Discovery required**
Fundamental questions remain unresolved and could materially affect the Design.

The work should not be considered ready until those questions have been addressed or an explicit decision has been made to proceed with the known uncertainty.

## 7. What happens when Design discovers something new?

Ready for Design is not intended to prevent Design from discovering new information.

New information is expected.

However, there is an important distinction between normal design discovery and discovering a fundamental unresolved service or product question.

For example:

**Normal design discovery:**

User research shows that people misunderstand an existing label.

Design can investigate, test and iterate on the solution.

**Fundamental service discovery:**

Design discovers that different publishing teams use the concepts of “edition” and “version” differently.

This may require a product or service decision before the design can meaningfully progress.

Where Design uncovers a fundamental question, the work should be assessed.

If the question could materially affect the direction, scope or validity of the design:

1. **Identify**
Document the question and what prompted it.

2. **Assess**
Determine whether it is a design question or a wider product, business, service, content or technical question.

3. **Pause where necessary**
If the question is fundamental, the Design work should move to Blocked / Discovery Required.

4. **Assign ownership**
The appropriate Product, Business, Service, Content or Technical owner should take responsibility for resolving the question.

Design can contribute to this discovery, but should not automatically become the owner of the underlying service decision.

5. **Resolve**
The decision, rationale and any resulting changes to requirements should be documented.

6. **Return to Design**
Design resumes once there is sufficient clarity to continue.

This creates a feedback loop rather than treating Ready for Design as a one-way gate.

## 8. Jira implementation

A Ready for Design Jira template should provide a consistent way of capturing the information required for the checkpoint.

The template should cover:
* User need
* Problem
* Scope
* User Stories
* Acceptance criteria
* Business and service rules
* Known variations
* Content and data
* Technical constraints
* Dependencies
* Outstanding decisions
* Decision owners

Not every field will apply to every piece of work.

The Jira template should therefore distinguish between information that is always required and information that is required where applicable.

Completion of the template does not automatically mean that work is ready.

The Design Readiness Check provides an opportunity to challenge assumptions, identify gaps and confirm that the appropriate people agree that the work is ready.

## 9. Where this applies

Ready for Design should apply to work entering the Design process where unresolved product, service, content, business or technical questions could materially affect the outcome.

This can include:
* Dataset experiences
* Findability
* Content work
* New features and journeys
* Changes to existing services
* Reusable templates and patterns
* Cross-service experiences
* Work involving multiple teams or publishing processes
* Other work where Design is expected to make significant user experience decisions

The process should be proportionate to the complexity and risk of the work.

A small change should not require the same level of discovery as a cross-service transformation.

## 10. Principles

**Understand before designing**
Design should begin with a shared understanding of the problem, users and service.

**Make uncertainty visible**
Unknowns are acceptable. Hidden unknowns are a risk.

**Don't make Design the default owner of unresolved service questions**
Design should contribute to discovery without automatically inheriting ownership of product, business or service decisions.

**Discovery can continue during Design**
Ready for Design is not a claim that everything is known.

**Stop when necessary**
If Design uncovers a fundamental unresolved question, it is appropriate to pause and resolve it rather than designing around an assumption.

**Keep it proportionate**
The process should support delivery, not become a barrier to it.

## 11. What good looks like

A successful Ready for Design process means that when a designer begins work, they can answer:
* What problem are we solving?
* Who are we solving it for?
* What do we know about their needs?
* How does the service work?
* What rules and constraints do I need to understand?
* What variations and exceptions do I need to account for?
* What decisions have already been made?
* What remains uncertain?
* Who owns those uncertainties?

And most importantly:

**What am I being asked to solve through Design, and what has already been established before I start?**

The goal is not to make Design predictable.

The goal is to ensure that Design is spending its time solving design problems, rather than discovering fundamental product and service requirements by accident.
